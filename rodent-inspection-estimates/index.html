What the technician checks.

An inspection follows the evidence. The technician begins wherever you noticed a sign and then works in widening circles, because the visible mess is rarely the whole story.

  • Roofline, soffits, ridge and gable vents, and any place a pipe or cable passes through.
  • Attic or crawl space, with attention to insulation that looks flattened or stained.
  • Garage door seals, walls, shelving and stored goods.
  • Foundation, slab edge, skirting and vents.
  • Yard: burrows, woodpiles, feeders, fruit trees and fences touching the building.

Photographs are taken as the walk proceeds, so the final report can show you exactly where each opening or dropping cluster sits.

Naming the animal.

A mouse and a rat need different traps, different metal and different cleanup. Droppings, rub marks, tooth marks and sound help tell them apart. Mice leave tiny pellets and gnaw small holes. Rats leave larger droppings and gnaw bigger ones, and roof rats climb where Norway rats dig.

If neither animal fits, the report says so. Bats, squirrels and raccoons need different handling, and we would rather tell you than treat the wrong problem.

Mapping the way in.

Every inspection ends with a list of entry points. A short sketch or numbered photographs show which ones are active, which are old and which can wait. That ranking matters when a budget is tight.

Before you call: public health advice, boiled down

Agencies largely agree on the cleaning method, so the shared steps are summarized here.

  • Old pellets turn to powder under a bristle, so mist them with disinfectant before a hand or towel gets close.
  • Prop the doors wide, let the breeze work and come back after roughly thirty minutes.
  • Skin contact with droppings, urine or nesting material is avoidable, and gloves make it so.
  • Cover the waste and its surroundings with disinfectant and let it rest before the first wipe.
  • Tie the bag, bin it and wash your hands once more.
  • Illness after cleaning up waste is a job for a physician, not an exterminator, so describe what you handled.

Inspection questions.

How much time should I set aside?

Most houses need under two hours, and a big lot or a cramped attic stretches that.

Must I be home for it?

An adult should be present to open up and field questions, but the outside walk can begin without you.

What if you find nothing?

We tell you so plainly. Pipes, wind and other animals make noise too, and when no rodents turn up we recommend no work.

Will the estimate be in writing?

Yes. It lists each stage and its price, so you can accept some items and decline others.

Can I book an inspection for a rental or a commercial space?

Yes. Tell the dispatcher who manages the building, since access and approvals sometimes involve more than one person.
