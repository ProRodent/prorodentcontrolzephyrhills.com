Garages, Storage and Rodents in Zephyrhills, FL

Garages are shady, cluttered and often not sealed well. This guide offers a weekend plan that removes most of what draws rodents in.

Written by Zephyrhills Pro Rodent Control, posted September 19, 2026. Reading time: roughly 4 minutes.

Ask most people which part of the house rodents visit first, and they say the kitchen. In practice, it is often the garage. A garage has a big door with a seal that ages in the sun, a pile of boxes along the wall and food in the form of seed, pet kibble or a forgotten snack.

Why garages draw rodents.

The garage offers three things a rodent wants. Shade keeps animals cool in the heat, clutter gives them cover and soft material makes a nest. Add a gap under the door and you have an open invitation.

In Zephyrhills summers, afternoon rain can blow under a door and soak cardboard on the floor. That damp pile becomes a prime nesting spot, and it can also grow mildew.

A weekend plan.

Plan on a few hours on a morning when the garage is cooler. Work in this order.

  • Clear a lane beside the walls so the base of each one is visible.
  • Move anything stacked on the floor onto shelves.
  • Swap cardboard for lidded plastic tubs, and label them.
  • Move pet food, seed and fertilizer into tight bins.
  • Sweep the floor with a damp mop, not a broom, if you see dust or droppings.
  • Check the door seal and corners, then the wall-to-slab line.

If you find droppings, stop and read the safety steps below. Wet them, wipe them and bag the waste, wearing gloves. Do not sweep.

A simple flashlight test.

Close the garage door, switch off the lights and look at the edges. Any thin line of daylight is a gap. Press on the seal to see whether it springs back or stays flat. Check the bottom corners especially, since that is where rodents squeeze in.

Heat and the garage.

A closed Florida garage gets very hot in summer, which is hard on stored items and on animals too. Rodents often settle at the cooler edges, near the floor and along shaded walls. Opening the door for a few hours on a dry day also lets the room air out.

Sealing the room.

A new bottom seal and tight corners handle the biggest gaps. Utility openings need mesh and sealant. Read the garage control page for the full checklist, and exclusion for the metals. If animals are present, trapping comes first, as described on the trapping page.

Signs the garage already has a tenant.

Look for pellets at the foot of the walls, shredded cardboard behind boxes and oily smears near door corners. New pellets look dark and moist, and old ones turn gray and crumble.

A tenant often leaves a trail from the door to the shelf. Follow it with a flashlight, and note where it ends. That spot is where a technician will start.

Keep it that way.

Set a calendar reminder for the first of each month. Spend five minutes walking the garage with a flashlight, lifting a few lids and checking the seal. Early signs are far cheaper to handle than a full infestation, and a quick inspection can confirm that you caught them in time.

Before you call, put the broom down

Agencies largely agree on the cleaning method, so the shared steps are summarized here.

  • No dry sweeping, no dry dusting, no dry anything: the rule is to wet first.
  • A room shut for weeks needs about thirty minutes of through-draft before anyone goes in to work.
  • Treat everything soiled as dirty until proven otherwise: gloves on, hands washed after, no exceptions.
  • Saturate the pellets and the surface under them using disinfectant, or bleach diluted in water, and leave it be for about five minutes.
  • Keep carcasses away from bare hands, bag them and place the bag in the trash.
  • If fever, body aches or labored breathing show up within the next few weeks, tell your doctor about the exposure. A pest company cannot give medical advice.

Reader questions.

Do Florida homes have basements?

True basements are uncommon here. Garages, utility rooms and sheds fill that role, and the advice below fits them.

Is cardboard really a problem?

Yes. Rodents shred it for bedding, and humidity softens it. Plastic tubs with lids are better.

Should I replace the door seal?

If it is cracked or flat, yes. It also keeps out rain and insects. Check the corners too.

Where should pet food go?

In a tight metal or hard plastic bin, and indoors if possible.

Can I do this myself?

Mostly yes. Close any gap you can, and call if you find droppings, nests or fresh gnawing.
