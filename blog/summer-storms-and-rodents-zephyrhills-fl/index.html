Summer Storms and Rodents in Zephyrhills, FL

Rain arrives like clockwork in a Florida summer. Whether it sends rodents your way depends less on the sky than on the state of your walls, and this guide shows how to check them.

Written by Zephyrhills Pro Rodent Control, posted October 4, 2026. Reading time: roughly 5 minutes.

Picture a typical July day in east Pasco County. Heat builds all morning, thunderheads climb after lunch, and by three o'clock the yard is under an inch of water. Rodents live in that yard too. What the downpour does to them is more modest than folklore says, and more important than most owners assume.

Underground trouble after a downpour.

Norway rats and many mice dig. A burrow that floods is no longer a refuge, so its owner leaves in a hurry. The nearest dry, roofed spot might be beneath a deck, inside a shed or against the garage wall.

Roof rats are different. They nest overhead, so floodwater rarely bothers them, but a gale can strip nests from palm crowns and force them to look for sturdier cover. Sometimes that cover is your attic.

We cannot forecast any storm, and we will not claim that calls jump after every squall. The narrower truth is enough: wind and water open fresh gaps and scatter old hiding places.

Your lap around the building the next morning.

Hold off until downed lines and shaky limbs stop being a hazard. After that, circle the outside slowly with a flashlight, low spots first and high ones next.

  • Examine the foot of every wall for new holes, crumbled soil or piled debris.
  • Press on vent covers and foundation screens, looking for dents, tears and curled corners.
  • Scan the roof edge for fronds or limbs lying against shingles.
  • Test the garage door gasket and each lower corner of the door.
  • Mark any water now standing against the slab.

Snap pictures of whatever you notice and note the date. If scratching starts weeks later, those pictures help us work out where the animals came in.

Hauling storm debris without stirring up trouble.

Cleanup after a blow can disturb nests and old pellets. Put on gloves, keep dust down and send children and pets elsewhere. Should you uncover droppings under a fallen limb, do not sweep them. The CDC advises soaking them with disinfectant and wiping, as the safety box below describes.

If a nest breaks open, back away and give the animal room. A frightened rodent bolts, and a cornered one will bite.

Two walks beat one.

See the rainy season as a cycle and not a crisis. Give the exterior one afternoon before the rains set in, then repeat a shorter lap after any big storm. Comparing the two is the point, because a new hole jumps out when you recall last month's wall.

  • Photograph every side of the building before the rainy months begin.
  • Take the same shots after any storm with strong wind or flooding.
  • Keep a brief note on loose screens, cracked seals and places where water gathers.
  • Fix small things promptly, before the next system arrives.

No special gear is required. Bring a flashlight, use a ladder carefully from the ground and spend a few minutes per side. Roof work is a roofer's job, and wet or windy weather is never the time to climb.

Damp stuff inside.

Moisture lingers after storms in attics, closets and garages. Soaked insulation, wet cardboard and damp wood hold odor and draw animals hunting for soft bedding. Dry or toss wet items fast. Damp boxes on a garage floor become ideal bedding when they dry, so swap them for lidded plastic tubs.

Closing up before the next front.

The least dramatic step helps the most: shut gaps while the weather is calm. Metal flashing and heavy mesh hold against teeth, and foam or thin plastic does not. Materials are listed on the exclusion page, while the food and shelter that attract animals to a property are covered in our yard work guide.

If wind has hurt the roof, soffits or siding, fix that first. A new gap at the roof edge is exactly what a roof rat discovers within a night or two.

When it is time to phone.

Phone us on finding pellets, new gnaw marks or fresh holes, or on hearing steps above you at night. An inspection works out which animal you have and where it came through. Report any storm damage, loose vent covers and debris, so our search has a starting point.

Before you call: dust, droppings and your lungs

Agencies largely agree on the cleaning method, so the shared steps are summarized here.

  • Dry waste is hazardous mainly as dust, so no sweeping, no vacuuming and no leaf blower anywhere near it.
  • Shut-up garages, sheds and attics hold stale air, so crack them open and step away for thirty minutes first.
  • Treat everything soiled as dirty until proven otherwise: gloves on, hands washed after, no exceptions.
  • Wet it thoroughly, wait a few minutes, then lift the mess with paper towels.
  • One tied-off bag is enough for towels and a carcass together, and a second bag helps if the plastic is thin.
  • Watch yourself for a few weeks, and call your doctor if flu-like symptoms or hard breathing appear.

Asked and answered.

Does a big storm push rodents into houses?

It can shift where they shelter. Saturated soil and flooded burrows may send them looking for dry cover, but only a gap in your building lets them in.

When is the best time to walk the property after rain?

As soon as conditions are safe, preferably within a day or two, while new holes and loose screens are still clear and before the grass regrows.

My ceiling has a brown stain. Is that a rodent sign?

Nearly always it is water. Count droppings, bite marks and night noises as separate evidence, and ask a roofer to trace any leak.

Can leaves and limbs hide a burrow?

Yes. A heap of fronds or boards against a wall can cover a new hole entirely. Rake it back, then look again.

Does sealing before the wet months help?

It does. Every gap closed in advance is one fewer door for an animal to use once the ground turns soggy.
