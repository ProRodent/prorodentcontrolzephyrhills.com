The garage as a side door.

A garage door usually wears a rubber strip along the bottom, and sunlight cracks it. Corners where the door meets its frame and holes where pipes or wires cross the wall add to the list. Any one can admit a mouse, and larger ones can admit a rat.

Once inside, rodents find what they need. Shelving offers hiding places, totes supply nesting material, and a bag of seed or pet food is dinner. The garage connects to the house in many cases, so a problem that begins here can spread.

A flashlight check you can do tonight.

Switch off the garage lights and shut the door. Crouch and look for daylight at the bottom and corners. Wherever light leaks, an animal can probably squeeze through.

  • Check the bottom seal for cracks or flat spots.
  • Look at the corners where the door meets the frame.
  • Run a light along the base of the walls for chewing or droppings.
  • Inspect shelves and the tops of boxes for nests and pellets.
  • Look behind a spare refrigerator, water heater or workbench.

Closing the room.

Sealing a garage means a new bottom seal and tight corners, with metal over any wall gap. Utility openings are packed with mesh and sealed. See entry point sealing for the full method.

Trapping comes first if animals are present. Stations go behind shelving and along the door wall, out of reach of pets.

Heat and storms in a garage.

Summer afternoons turn a closed garage into an oven, and thunderstorms can blow rain under the door. Rodents seek shade and dry shelter, so both conditions raise the odds of a visit. A good seal does double duty by keeping out water and animals.

A simple habit that pays off.

Once a month, spend five minutes walking the room. Look at the seal, scan the floor for droppings and lift a few boxes. Early signs are much cheaper to deal with than a full colony.

When you find droppings, follow the CDC cleanup steps in the safety box on this page, and call for an inspection if activity looks fresh.

Before you call: a careful routine for pellets and nests

Return to this list each time pellets, urine marks or torn nesting appear indoors.

  • No dry sweeping, no dry dusting, no dry anything: the rule is to wet first.
  • Prop the doors wide, let the breeze work and come back after roughly thirty minutes.
  • Skin contact with droppings, urine or nesting material is avoidable, and gloves make it so.
  • Cover the waste and its surroundings with disinfectant and let it rest before the first wipe.
  • Seal the bag, then drop it in the regular garbage.
  • We do not give medical advice, and anyone who feels sick after contact with rodent waste should see a clinician.

Garage and storage questions.

Do Florida homes have basements?

True basements are uncommon here. Utility rooms, sheds, storage closets and attached garages stand in, and this page covers all of them.

Why do rodents nest in garages?

Garages offer shade, clutter and often a gap under the door. Totes, cardboard and bags of seed provide soft material and food.

Can I just replace the door seal?

A fresh seal helps, but rodents also use corners, wall gaps and utility openings. A walk-through finds the rest.

Is stored food a risk?

Yes. Pet food, bird seed and grass seed draw rodents. Tubs with tight lids reduce the risk.

What about a refrigerator in the garage?

Warm coils and wiring attract mice, and a cord can be chewed. Tell the technician if you have one, and keep it clear of clutter.
