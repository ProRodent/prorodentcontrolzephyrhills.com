Reading a Zephyrhills property.

East Pasco County, away from the Gulf shore, spends its summers hot, damp and thundery most afternoons. Weather moves rodents around. A flooded burrow sends its tenant hunting for a dry shelf, perhaps yours in the garage, and a scorching day drives animals into shade.

That means the useful question is not whether rodents live nearby, because some almost certainly do. Ask whether your building hands them an entrance. A short evening stroll with a torch tells you more than any amount of guessing.

Telling the animals apart.

A house mouse squeezes through a slit about the width of a pencil. Rats require something closer to half an inch. Roof rats travel high, along cables, fence rails and palm trunks. Norway rats stay low and tunnel next to slabs, sheds and footings.

Trap style, metal gauge and cleanup method all hinge on the species, which is why naming the animal precedes any quote. What you describe on the phone gives us a head start, and the property walk confirms it.

When the rains arrive.

Wet weather dominates from early summer through fall, with the occasional tropical system mixed in. Saturated soil pushes burrowers toward higher, drier shelter. Once a storm passes, walk a full circle around the house and look for fresh holes, vent covers that shifted and any limb or palm frond now lying on the roof.

  • Ahead of the rainy stretch, tug each vent cover and pipe collar, then inspect the bottom corners of the garage door.
  • Store pet kibble, wild bird seed and lawn seed in lidded tubs that snap shut.
  • Raise firewood and spare lumber onto a rack set away from the walls.
  • Trim any palm frond or limb that touches the roof or siding.

Until the technician arrives.

Three small choices protect both your household and the clues we need. Do not sweep up pellets, send kids and animals elsewhere, and leave any trap you put out in its spot.

  • Take pictures of pellets and gnaw marks, placing a coin next to each to show size.
  • Jot the room and the time every time you hear a sound.
  • Close off whichever room sees the most traffic.
  • Skip loose bait and sticky boards, because each creates a hazard of its own.

How a visit unfolds.

Whether the culprit is one pantry mouse or a nest in a pickup engine, the job runs through four stages in the same order.

  1. Look.

    Work begins at the spot with the strongest evidence, be it attic, garage, crawl space or yard, then follows the roofline looking for new droppings and the holes being used.

  2. Catch.

    Traps are placed along the lanes the clues mark, matched to the species and positioned out of reach of kids and pets.

  3. Close.

    Every crack, vent and gnawed hole is covered with flashing, wire mesh or a sealant hard enough to stop teeth.

  4. Confirm.

    We come back to empty the traps, tug on each seal and take on whatever cleanup or repair is still pending.

Signs that rodents are inside.

Two or more of these clustered in one corner usually mean this week's visitors, not last year's leftovers.

  • Droppings.

    Tiny dark pellets under a sink, along a baseboard, on a garage shelf or scattered over attic insulation.

  • Fresh chewing.

    Fresh bites look light and ragged, whether on a rafter, a plastic pipe, a wire cover or a cardboard box.

  • Sounds after dark.

    Scrabbling, light patter or a dull knock inside a wall or over a ceiling after the household settles.

  • Nesting scraps.

    A ball of torn paper, tuggedloose insulation, fabric scraps or dry palm fiber, stuffed into a sheltered corner.

  • Rub marks.

    Dark, greasy marks on walls, pipes and joists show where something travels each night.

  • A bad smell.

    A sharp ammonia note in a closed room, or a heavy, sweet rot where an animal has died.

Recognize a couple of these? Phone us before more damage piles up.

Repair any dripping outdoor spigot, because a wet patch beside a wall attracts rodents and insects, and mention one if you spot it.

Why your surroundings change the plan.

Knowing the nearest landmark lets us picture the area, but it never shows what is nesting in your walls. A hushed residential lane, a lot facing open ground and a restaurant beside a loading bay would each have us begin somewhere different.

Count vehicles too. Anything parked for weeks, such as a pickup, camper, boat trailer or golf cart, can become a nest, so pop the hood and smell the cabin vents after you have been away. Spotting a bitten wire early costs far less than replacing a harness.

Houses left empty for months.

Plenty of Florida houses stand vacant for part of the year, and a silent house tempts rodents. Before you depart, clear the pantry of opened food, close gaps around the garage door and ask someone to peek inside periodically. On your return, glance at the attic hatch before you unpack.

First questions, short answers.

Do you answer calls at night?

Yes, around the clock and on holidays. If the day has an opening we offer a same-day visit, and if not you hear the next available slot.

How do I know whether it is mice or rats?

Size and location offer hints. Rice-grain pellets lean toward mice, and bigger blunt ones toward rats. Heavy sounds overhead at dusk suggest roof rats. We confirm on site.

Should I put out poison first?

We advise against it. An animal that eats bait often dies in a wall void, and the resulting stench is tougher to fix than the first problem. The EPA page linked below covers the rules.
