What a parked vehicle offers a rodent.

An engine compartment stays warm for hours after parking and is stuffed with soft hose wrap and insulation. To a mouse or rat looking for shelter, it is a ready-made den. Shade and open garages here add to the appeal.

Long storage is the biggest risk. A truck that sits for a month while you travel, or a golf cart idle through summer, gives animals time to settle in.

Signs to look for.

  • Pellets or shredded material on the engine cover, battery tray or inside the air box.
  • Chewed wire jackets, hoses or insulation.
  • A burning smell when you start the engine.
  • A musty smell or visible debris in the cabin vents.
  • Warning lights that appear for no clear reason.

If any of these appear, do not start the vehicle until it has been inspected. A short circuit or a nest near hot parts can cause real damage.

Clearing the nest.

The technician opens the hood and checks the air filter box, battery area, wheel wells and under-dash spaces. Nests and food stashes are removed, and affected surfaces are wetted and wiped to keep dust down.

Traps or stations may go nearby if animals are still present. The technician also checks where the vehicle is parked, since the source is often a nearby garage, shed or hedge.

Keeping vehicles safe.

  • Park away from woodpiles, dense shrubs and sheds when you can.
  • Start and move a stored vehicle every few weeks.
  • Keep food, pet treats and seed out of cars and trucks.
  • Lift the hood and sniff the cabin vents after any long absence.

If your garage is the source, see garage control and exclusion. For odor from a dead animal in a vehicle, see the odor page.

Before you call: handling waste without raising dust

Thinking about tidying up before a technician comes? Follow the sequence shown, and stop wherever a step feels unclear.

  • Put the vacuum away too, because even a filtered one can push the finest particles back into the room.
  • Shut-up garages, sheds and attics hold stale air, so crack them open and step away for thirty minutes first.
  • Skin contact with droppings, urine or nesting material is avoidable, and gloves make it so.
  • Spray, pause, wipe: that is the rhythm, with roughly five minutes in the middle.
  • Used towels go into a plastic bag, and so does any dead rodent, handled with gloves or a tool and never bare fingers.
  • If fever, body aches or labored breathing show up within the next few weeks, tell your doctor about the exposure. A pest company cannot give medical advice.

Vehicle questions.

What makes a vehicle a good nest site?

An engine bay is warm and hidden, and it is packed with soft insulation. A truck that stands for weeks reads as a quiet shelter.

Can rodents damage wiring?

Yes. They chew wire jackets and hoses, which can cause electrical faults or leaks. A mechanic should inspect any damage.

Is it safe to start a car with a nest?

It is a gamble. A bitten wire can short, and a nest by the exhaust can smolder. Have the vehicle inspected before driving.

Do you fix the wires?

No. We clear the animals and nesting material. A mechanic repairs electrical or mechanical damage.

What about boats and golf carts?

Yes, we check them too. Cabin vents, battery boxes and covers are common hiding places.
