A few good spots beat many bad ones.

Twelve traps in poor locations catch nothing, while three well placed ones can clear a house. Rodents repeat their routes nightly, hugging walls and racing along beams and pipes.

The technician reads droppings, rub marks and tooth marks, then places devices on those routes. That is why trapping follows inspection, and why a technician who skips the second step is mostly guessing.

Snap traps and enclosed stations.

Snap traps kill quickly and let the technician see every catch. Enclosed stations hold the mechanism in a box with a small opening, which keeps curious pets and children away. The choice depends on who lives in the building and where the traps will sit.

  • Attics: stations along rafters, near vents and by the hatch.
  • Garages: stations behind shelving and along the door wall.
  • Kitchens: low, against baseboards, never on open counters.
  • Outdoors: lockable stations by burrows, sheds and fence lines.

Pre-baiting and patience.

Rats are wary of anything new. Traps often sit unset for a day or two so the animals get used to them. This feels slow, but it raises catch rates and shortens the whole job.

Rechecks, logs and a finish line.

Each visit counts catches, resets or swaps traps and looks for new signs. Work ends when traps stay empty for the agreed period and no fresh droppings appear. The dates are set at the start, so you are never left guessing.

Trapping is only half the job.

Without sealing, new animals replace the old ones. That is why trapping and entry point sealing are paired. The order matters too. Close holes only after the building is clear, or an animal can be trapped inside a wall.

If the animals have nested in the attic, cleanup comes after trapping, and any smell from a death in the wall is handled on the odor page.

Safe disposal and your health.

Dead rodents are lifted with gloves and sealed in bags. Anything they touched is dampened with disinfectant and wiped, never swept. We follow the CDC steps given in this page's safety box, and anyone who feels unwell afterward should see a doctor.

Before you call, safe cleanup in plain steps

No phone call is required to follow these steps. They match what health agencies tell anyone who finds droppings.

  • Old pellets turn to powder under a bristle, so mist them with disinfectant before a hand or towel gets close.
  • Shut-up garages, sheds and attics hold stale air, so crack them open and step away for thirty minutes first.
  • Hands are the main route between waste and your mouth, so gloves go on first and soap comes last.
  • Saturate the pellets and the surface under them using disinfectant, or bleach diluted in water, and leave it be for about five minutes.
  • One tied-off bag is enough for towels and a carcass together, and a second bag helps if the plastic is thin.
  • Persistent fever or shortness of breath after an exposure should be checked by a physician.

Trapping questions.

Why not use poison?

Poisoned rodents often crawl into walls or ducts and die there. Loose bait also puts pets, children and wildlife at risk. The EPA page below explains the limits.

Are glue boards a good option?

We avoid them. They catch lizards and birds, they cause suffering and they leave a mess to clean.

How many visits does trapping take?

Often two to four, depending on how many animals are present and how fast the catches stop.

Are the traps safe around my dog?

Enclosed stations and placement along walls keep devices out of reach. Tell us about any dog that roams.

Where do the animals end up?

Gloved hands lift them out and seal them in bags for disposal, in line with the CDC steps on this page.
