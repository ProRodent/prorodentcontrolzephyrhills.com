Look at your edges.

Rodents travel along edges: a fence line, a wall, a drainage ditch, the line where lawn meets brush. If your property touches open ground of any kind, those edges deserve a look.

  • Walk the fence and look for burrow holes and smooth runways.
  • Check the base of sheds and the gap between floor and ground.
  • Look at the line where lawn meets brush for trails.
  • Notice if droppings appear near the trash cans or pet bowls.

Garage first.

A garage is often the easiest door. Check the bottom seal, the corners and any wall gap. Move cardboard onto shelves in lidded tubs and keep seed and pet food in tight containers. See garage control for the full checklist.

Pets, feeders and fruit.

Outdoor pet dishes, bird feeders and fruit trees give rodents a meal. Bring dishes in at dusk, place a tray under feeders and pick up fruit often. These changes are cheap and often cut activity quickly.

Storm season.

After a storm, look at the foundation for new holes and at the roof edge for loose vents. Heavy rain can flood burrows and drive animals toward dry spots in a garage or shed. Rake debris away from the walls so you can see what is there.

Before you call: public health advice, boiled down

The notes below restate guidance from public health agencies. They apply to any household, whether or not a visit is ever booked.

  • A quick sweep feels harmless, yet it can launch particles that hang in still air for a long time.
  • Fresh air first, cleanup second: thirty minutes with the windows open is the CDC's starting point for closed spaces.
  • Keep gloves on through the whole task, and keep your hands away from your face while you wear them.
  • Contact time counts, so use plenty of disinfectant and let it sit on the waste before you wipe.
  • Seal the bag, then drop it in the regular garbage.
  • Persistent fever or shortness of breath after an exposure should be checked by a physician.
